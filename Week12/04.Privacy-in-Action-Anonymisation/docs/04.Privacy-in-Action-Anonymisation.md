# Data Privacy, De-Identification & $k$-Anonymity

Under APP 11 of the *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth), entities holding personal information must take reasonable steps to protect it from misuse, interference, loss, and unauthorized access. When data is shared for secondary research, it must be robustly de-identified.

---

## 1. The Threat of Linkage Attacks (Sweeney's Theorem)

In 1997, computer scientist Dr. Latanya Sweeney proved that simply removing names and Social Security numbers does not protect privacy:

> **87% of the US population** can be uniquely identified by the combination of just three quasi-identifiers: **{5-digit ZIP code, Gender, Date of Birth}**.

By linking "anonymised" health records with publicly available voter registration records, individuals can be readily re-identified.

## 2. Defence Mechanisms

### A. Cryptographic Salting & Pseudonymisation
Raw hashing (`SHA-256(student_id)`) can be cracked using precomputed rainbow tables. Salting appends an unpredictable, high-entropy secret string before hashing:

$$\text{Token} = \text{SHA-256}(\text{Salt} \mathbin{\Vert} \text{Identifier})$$

### B. $k$-Anonymity
A dataset satisfies $k$-anonymity if each unique combination of quasi-identifiers belongs to an equivalence class of size at least $k$:

$$\forall q \in Q, \quad |\{r \in D \mid r[Q] = q\}| \ge k$$

To achieve $k$-anonymity:
1. **Generalisation / Binning**: Replace exact numbers with ranges (e.g. age $23 \to [20, 29]$).
2. **Coarsening**: Truncate codes (e.g. postcode $2060 \to 20\text{XX}$).
3. **Suppression**: Redact extreme outliers that cannot be grouped.
